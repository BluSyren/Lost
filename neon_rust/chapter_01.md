# Chapter 1: Dead Tech

The air in the Sump always tasted like ozone and burnt copper. It was the smell of a million fried circuits and the recycled breath of the city above settling into the gutter.

Jax wiped a smear of grease from his cheek with a hand that whirred softly. His left arm, a bulky matte-black model from the War of the Three Corporations, was twitching again. The servos in the wrist locked, then released with a hiss of hydraulics.

"Piece of junk," he muttered, picking up a sonic screwdriver.

He was hunched over a workbench in a cramped shop that doubled as his apartment. Shelves lined the walls, stacked with scavenged optics, fraying power cables, and bio-processors that had been illegal for a decade. Outside, the neon rain of Sector 4 sizzled against the pavement, the pink and blue light filtering through his grimy window.

A red warning light blinked on his HUD—the visual overlay projected directly onto his retina.

**[SYSTEM WARNING: Myo-Cardial Regulator at 12% Efficiency. Maintenance Required.]**

Jax swiped the notification away. "Yeah, yeah. I know."

He knew exactly what a new regulator cost. Three thousand credits. He had forty-two in his account. Enough for a week of synthetic noodles and maybe a charge for his arm. Not enough to keep his heart beating for another month.

The chime of the door sensor cut through the drone of the ventilation fan.

Jax didn't look up. "Closed. Unless you have a pristine T-400 power cell, in which case, I'm open."

"Better," a voice said. Smooth. Synthetic velvet.

Jax froze. He knew that voice. He set the screwdriver down and turned slowly.

Leaning against the doorframe was Silas. The Fixer wore a suit that cost more than Jax’s entire shop—shimmering silk-weave that changed color with the viewing angle. Currently, it was a predatory shade of violet.

"Silas," Jax grunted. "I thought you were dead."

"Optimism is a flaw, Jax. You should know that." Silas stepped inside, wrinkling his nose at the smell of oil and old sweat. "I have a job."

"I'm retired."

"You're dying," Silas corrected, tapping his own chest. "I can see the bio-telemetry from here. That regulator is thrashing like a fish on dry land."

Jax narrowed his eyes. "If you're scanning me, I'll rip your optics out."

"Touchy. Look, this is simple. A transport job. Pick up a package in Sector 7, drop it off in Sector 9. No questions, no peeking."

"Sector 7 is Corp territory," Jax said, turning back to his bench. "I don't run High-Gen anymore. My gear can't handle the ICE."

"No hacking required. Just a physical pickup. Hard copy data."

"Why me? You have a thousand kids with slick chrome legs who would kill for this."

"Because those kids are loud. They boost fast and leave trails," Silas said, stepping closer. "I need a ghost. I need someone who uses analog routes. Someone who knows the old tunnels."

Jax looked at his twitching metal hand. He clenched it into a fist. The servos whined in protest.

"How much?"

Silas smiled, a sharp, white expression in the dim light. "Five thousand credits. Plus a military-grade regulator. The kind they put in Shock Troopers."

Jax’s heart—the meat part—skipped a beat. That was a payout that could buy him five years of life. Maybe ten.

"Who's the target?" Jax asked.

"Not a target. A transfer. You meet the contact at the intersection of 4th and Helix. Midnight. Blue umbrella."

"That's in two hours."

"Then you better get moving." Silas dropped a credit chip on the messy workbench. "Half now. Half on delivery."

Jax stared at the chip. It was heavy, black plastic with gold contacts. It looked like a tombstone.

He snatched it up.

"If this is a setup, Silas," Jax said, his voice dropping to a gravelly growl, "I will find you. And I won't use the door."

Silas didn't blink. "Midnight, Jax. Don't be late."

The Fixer turned and vanished into the rain, leaving the door sliding shut behind him.

Jax stood alone in the hum of his shop. The warning light blinked on his HUD again.

**[11% Efficiency.]**

He grabbed his trench coat from the hook. It was heavy, reinforced with kevlar weave. He slid his arms in, the leather creaking. He checked the heavy pistol mag-locked to his thigh—an old projectile thrower, loud and messy, but it punched through energy shields like they were paper.

"One last run," he told the empty room.

He didn't believe it. But he didn't have a choice.

He killed the lights and stepped out into the neon flood.
