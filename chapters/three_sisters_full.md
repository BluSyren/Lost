## CHAPTER ONE: THE FESTIVAL BEGINS

The sun rose over Kituhwa like a promise kept.

From above, the Confederated Nations sprawled across the valley in harmonious contradiction—sleek solar panels crowned buildings shaped like ancient longhouses, wind turbines turned beside ceremonial grounds, and everywhere, *everywhere*, things grew. Rooftop gardens cascaded down the sides of apartment complexes. Community plots burst with vegetables between residential blocks. The Three Sisters crops—corn, beans, and squash—climbed and sprawled and stood tall across hundreds of acres of sacred fields that formed the green heart of the nation.

Two million people called Kituhwa home. Two million people who had proven that the old ways and new possibilities weren't enemies. Two million people who were about to be tested.

Ayanna Osiris stood barefoot in the Sacred Fields, her toes pressed into rich soil that remembered a thousand plantings. The earth hummed beneath her feet—not a sound, but a sensation that traveled up through her legs and settled in her chest like a second heartbeat. Her gift. Her burden. Her purpose.

She wore her ceremonial clothes: a wraparound skirt the color of autumn corn, dyed with traditional methods her grandmother had taught her before the old woman passed. Over it, a fitted vest of modern tactical fabric, because Aya was nothing if not practical. Her hair hung in a single thick braid down her back, woven with golden beads and corn silk. The morning breeze carried the scent of turned earth and growing things.

Around her, dozens of families gathered for the opening ceremony of the Three Sisters Festival. Children darted between adult legs, their laughter bright as birdsong. Elders moved slowly to their places, wrapped in blankets despite the warmth, their presence anchoring everything. Young couples held hands. Grandparents lifted toddlers onto their shoulders for a better view.

Aya closed her eyes and placed both palms flat against the ground.

The Root Network opened.

Golden light spider-webbed from her hands, invisible to most but clear as day to her inner sight. Her consciousness traveled through the earth like water finding its path, spreading outward in every direction. She felt the Festival crowd—their weight on the ground, the rhythm of their heartbeats translated through vibration, the warmth of their bodies heating the soil. She felt the crops growing in their sacred patterns: corn stalks standing sentinel, bean vines climbing upward in spirals, squash leaves spreading wide to shade the ground. She felt the health of the land itself, the richness of soil that had been tended with ceremony and care for generations.

She felt the sickness, too.

There. To the east, where the new housing development was going up. The earth felt... hollow. Like soil that had forgotten why it mattered. Like ground that didn't know who walked on it anymore.

Aya's eyes snapped open, her jaw tight.

"Aya!" Ki's voice cut through her concentration. "You're doing the thing again."

Kiona Blackwater appeared at Aya's side, somehow managing to look both traditional and contemporary in her layered outfit—a ribbon shirt in deep green over fitted leggings, her natural hair twisted with bean seeds and emerald beads. At twenty-five, Ki was all curves and bright energy, her smile quick and her empathy quicker. She touched Aya's arm gently.

"The ceremony's about to start," Ki said. "You need to come back from wherever your roots took you."

Aya straightened, brushing soil from her palms. "Did you feel it?"

"Feel what?"

"The eastern quarter. Something's wrong with the bonds there."

Ki's expression shifted, her gift awakening. Her eyes unfocused slightly as she opened herself to the connections between people. To Ki, human relationships were visible as threads of light—green, glowing, beautiful. When people loved each other, the threads shone bright. When bonds frayed, they dimmed and thinned.

She looked east, toward the new development, and her breath caught.

"Oh," she whispered. "Oh no."

The threads over there weren't just dim. They were severed. Cut clean through, leaving raw ends that flickered like dying stars. Families living in those new houses, separated from the community by more than just distance. The bonds between them and the rest of Kituhwa, sliced apart.

"When did that happen?" Ki asked, her voice strained.

"Last night, I think. Maybe this morning." Aya's hands curled into fists. "We need to tell the council."

"After the ceremony," Ki said firmly. "We have a responsibility here first."

Before Aya could argue, a whistle cut through the air—sharp, commanding, unmistakably Zola.

Zola Runningbear prowled the perimeter of the gathering like a panther guarding her territory. At twenty-two, she was the youngest of the Three Sisters and the most immediately dangerous. Compact muscle moved beneath dark purple tactical gear. Her fade was fresh, geometric patterns shaved into the sides. The scarification on her shoulders—traditional Choctaw designs representing squash vines—showed at the edges of her sleeveless shirt.

She saw everything. The families settling into place. The elders taking their seats of honor. The children being corralled by patient parents. And more importantly, she saw the vulnerabilities. The unguarded approaches. The sightlines that could hide threats. The gaps in her protective coverage.

Purple energy shimmered at the edges of her vision—her gift activating automatically, spreading her protective sense across the Sacred Fields. Covering ground. That's what squash did. That's what Zola did.

She caught Aya's eye and gave a sharp nod. *Perimeter secure.*

Aya nodded back. *Begin.*

The ceremony started with the drumbeat.

Old Man Takoda, ninety-three and sharp as obsidian, struck the ceremonial drum with hands that had planted more corn than most people would see in their lifetimes. The sound rolled across the field, deep and primal, calling the gathered families to attention.

Aya stepped forward to the center of the planting circle.

"We gather today," she began, her voice carrying without amplification, "as we have gathered for generations. We plant the Three Sisters—corn, beans, and squash—not just as crops, but as a teaching. Not just as tradition, but as truth."

She knelt and began digging the first hole with her bare hands. The soil yielded easily, welcoming.

"Corn stands tall," Aya said, placing seeds carefully. "She provides structure. A foundation. She reaches toward the sky so that others might climb."

Golden light pulsed from the planted seeds—subtle, but those who knew how to look could see it. Roots spreading unnaturally fast, anchoring deep.

Ki stepped forward next, kneeling beside Aya's planted corn.

"Beans climb high," Ki said, her voice warm as summer. "She connects earth to sky, low to high, one to another. She feeds what she touches, making the soil richer for all."

She pressed bean seeds into the ground near the corn. Green light spiraled upward, and if you watched closely, you could see the bean sprouts already beginning to twine toward the corn stalks that would grow.

Zola was last, moving with controlled grace to complete the sacred triangle.

"Squash spreads wide," Zola said, her tone fierce with pride. "She covers the ground, protecting the roots of her sisters. She shades the earth, keeps moisture in, and lets no weeds take hold."

She planted squash seeds in a broad circle around corn and bean. Purple energy rippled outward like protective shields, and the leaves that would eventually grow were already promised in that light.

The three women stood together, hands joined over the planting.

"Alone, we struggle," they said in unison. "Together, we thrive. This is the way. This is the teaching. This is Kituhwa."

The gathered families echoed: "This is Kituhwa."

And then the celebration began—children rushing forward to plant their own Three Sisters gardens, elders supervising and teaching, food appearing as if by magic (though really it was just good community organization), and music rising from drums and flutes and voices that remembered songs older than the nation itself.

Aya felt the land respond to the ceremony. The soil, nourished by intention and care. The bonds between families, strengthened by shared purpose. The sacred balance, maintained for another season.

But underneath it all, that wrongness in the eastern quarter nagged at her like a splinter.

Ki felt it too, she knew. The way Ki's smile didn't quite reach her eyes. The way she kept glancing east, her gift clearly showing her those severed threads.

And Zola—Zola's protective field had tightened, drawn in closer. She sensed danger even if she couldn't name it yet.

The Three Sisters stood together in the Sacred Fields, surrounded by their thriving community, and felt the first tremors of something breaking.

Across the city, in the gleaming auditorium of the Innovation Center, another gathering was happening.

The crowd here was younger, dressed in contemporary fashion, holding tablets and phones instead of planting tools. The space was modern—floor-to-ceiling windows, minimalist design, chairs arranged in neat rows facing a raised stage. No drums here. Just the hum of climate control and the buzz of excited conversation.

A banner hung above the stage: **ASCENSION INITIATIVE: KITUHWA'S FUTURE BEGINS TODAY**

Marcus Salter stood backstage, checking his appearance in a full-length mirror. Tailored suit, deep charcoal with subtle patterns that evoked traditional designs without actually being traditional. Hair in neat locs tied back with red cord. He looked successful. Trustworthy. Like someone who had thought deeply about difficult questions and found answers.

He looked nothing like the broken thing he'd become.

In the mirror, if you knew how to see it, black ash swirled around his reflection like smoke that couldn't decide which way the wind was blowing.

"Five minutes, Mr. Salter," an aide said, poking her head through the curtain.

Marcus smiled. "Thank you, Demi."

He could hear the crowd beyond the curtain. Hundreds of people. Maybe a thousand. Young professionals frustrated by traditional hierarchies. Families struggling with the economic limitations of Kituhwa's deliberately modest growth model. Elders who'd watched their grandchildren leave for opportunities outside the nation. Artists who felt stifled by cultural expectations. Students who wanted to study at outside universities but faced community pressure to stay.

All of them carrying legitimate grievances.

All of them ready to hear what Marcus had to say.

He touched his chest—just over his heart—and felt the corruption pulse beneath his skin. The Blight. The thing that had taken root in him ten years ago when grief and doubt had made him vulnerable. The thing that fed on severed bonds and broken connections.

The thing that whispered in his mind with voices he'd learned to ignore: *Cut them loose. Free them. Make them understand that connection is weakness.*

Marcus took a breath and stepped through the curtain to thunderous applause.

The crowd rose to their feet, and he felt it—the intoxicating energy of people ready for change. Ready to believe. Ready to follow.

"Thank you," Marcus said, his voice amplified by hidden speakers. "Thank you for coming. Thank you for having the courage to ask difficult questions."

He paused, letting the moment breathe.

"Today, across the city, there's a festival happening. The Three Sisters Festival. A beautiful tradition. I grew up attending it, just like many of you. I learned the stories. I planted the seeds. I sang the songs."

He saw heads nodding. They remembered too.

"But I also remember asking questions. Why do we have to do it this way? Why can't we try new approaches? Why do the elders say no to every innovation that doesn't fit their vision of tradition?"

More nods. These questions resonated.

"And you know what they told me?" Marcus leaned forward, his voice dropping conspiratorially. "They told me I didn't understand. That I was too young. That if I truly respected the old ways, I wouldn't ask such questions."

Murmurs of recognition rippled through the crowd.

"Well." Marcus straightened, his smile sharp. "I'm not young anymore. And I'm still asking questions."

The screens behind him lit up with images: Kituhwa's beautiful landscapes, thriving communities, happy families. All true. All real.

"Kituhwa works," Marcus said. "No one denies that. We're healthier, wealthier, and more connected than the territories around us. Our model of blending traditional ecological knowledge with modern technology has been recognized globally."

The images shifted: awards, international recognition, diplomatic visits.

"But I want to ask you something." Marcus's voice turned serious. "Is it working because of tradition? Or despite it?"

Silence fell over the auditorium.

"What if," Marcus continued, "the things that make us successful—our agricultural innovation, our renewable energy, our community structures—what if those things could work even better without the weight of ceremony and ritual slowing us down?"

He gestured to the screens, which now showed comparative data: crop yields, economic indicators, time spent in ceremonial obligations versus productive labor.

"We spend thirty percent of our collective time on ceremonies. Thirty percent. Imagine what we could accomplish if we redirected that energy toward actual growth."

A young woman in the front row raised her hand. Marcus nodded to her.

"But doesn't the ceremony create the bonds that make us strong?" she asked. "Isn't that the point?"

Marcus smiled, and those watching closely might have seen black ash swirl in his eyes.

"That's what they tell us," he said gently. "That the ceremonies create bonds. That the rituals make us connected. But I ask you—do you need a ceremony to love your family? Do you need a ritual to care about your neighbors?"

He paused, letting the question sink in.

"Or are you connected because you *choose* to be? Because it serves you? Because it makes sense?"

The young woman frowned, uncertain.

Marcus pressed his advantage. "The Three Sisters tell us we need each other. That we can't survive alone. But isn't that just another way of saying we can't be trusted to make our own choices? That we're too weak to stand on our own?"

He could feel it now—his power activating, spreading through the crowd like invisible fingers. Finding the bonds between people and... testing them. Just testing. Seeing which ones were strong and which were vulnerable.

The vulnerable ones, he touched lightly. Just enough to plant doubt. Just enough to make people wonder if maybe, possibly, they didn't need each other quite as much as they thought.

Several people shifted uncomfortably in their seats, suddenly feeling a strange distance from the person sitting next to them.

"I'm not saying we should abandon community," Marcus said, his voice soothing now. "I'm saying we should choose it freely. Not because tradition demands it. Not because ceremony binds us. But because we genuinely want to."

The screens behind him shifted to show images of Kituhwa's few problems: the youth leaving for outside opportunities, the economic limitations, the occasional conflicts between traditional and progressive voices.

"These problems persist because we're not allowed to address them honestly. Every solution that challenges tradition gets rejected. Every innovation that doesn't fit the old ways gets dismissed. We're told to trust the elders. Trust the ceremonies. Trust the Three Sisters."

He paused.

"But what if they're wrong?"

The silence was absolute.

"What if the Three Sisters aren't protecting us?" Marcus said quietly. "What if they're holding us back?"

And there it was. The thing no one was supposed to say out loud.

"Tomorrow," Marcus continued, his voice growing stronger, "I'm going to perform a new ceremony. Not of planting, but of liberation. A ritual that will let every person in Kituhwa choose their bonds freely. Choose their obligations consciously. Choose their connections without coercion."

He spread his arms wide.

"I call it the Unbinding. And I invite all of you to witness it. To participate. To finally be free."

The crowd erupted—half in applause, half in shocked murmurs, all in confusion.

Marcus smiled and stepped back from the podium.

In the Sacred Fields, three miles away, Aya suddenly stumbled. Her connection to the earth screamed warning. Ki gasped, feeling a hundred bonds simultaneously weaken. Zola's protective field flickered as something massive and malevolent brushed against it.

The Three Sisters turned as one, looking toward the Innovation Center.

"What was that?" Ki whispered.

Aya's hands pressed to the ground, her gift searching frantically for the source of the disturbance.

"Something just cut through the bonds in the eastern quarter," Ki said, her voice shaking. "All of them. Every single one."

Zola's hands went to her weapons—though what good they'd do against a supernatural threat, she didn't know.

"We need to find out who's doing this," Aya said.

But in her heart, she already knew.

There was only one person in Kituhwa with that kind of power.

Only one person who would use it.

Marcus Salter.

And the real fight for Kituhwa's soul had just begun.

## CHAPTER TWO: THE COUNCIL MEETING

The Elder Council chamber sat at the heart of Kituhwa's administrative complex, a building that managed to be both traditional and contemporary in the way only Kituhwa could achieve. The walls were living wood—actual trees that had been guided and shaped over decades into a circular structure. Sunlight filtered through a glass ceiling inscribed with traditional patterns, casting geometric shadows across the council floor. The seats were arranged in concentric circles, with the eldest at the center and younger council members spiraling outward.

It was supposed to represent the ripples of wisdom spreading through generations.

Right now, it felt like a target.

Aya stood in the speaker's circle, flanked by Ki and Zola, facing twelve council members whose expressions ranged from concern to skepticism to outright hostility. The chamber smelled of sage and tension.

"You're saying Marcus Salter is using dark powers to sever bonds between people," Councilor Nakai said, his tone making it clear what he thought of that claim. He was fifty-three, successful in Kituhwa's sustainable tech sector, and increasingly sympathetic to calls for modernization. "That's a serious accusation."

"It's not an accusation," Aya said evenly. "It's a fact. I felt it through the Root Network. Ki saw it through her bonds. Zola's protective sense confirmed it. The eastern quarter has been cut off from the rest of Kituhwa. Spiritually severed."

She pulled up a holographic display—yes, Kituhwa had those, traditional didn't mean primitive—showing the mapped connections across the nation. The eastern quarter was a void. Black where there should have been light.

Elder Takoda leaned forward, his ancient eyes sharp despite his ninety-three years. "Show us the temporal comparison."

Aya swiped, and the display shifted to show yesterday's map. The eastern quarter had been normal then—full of bonds, connections glowing green and healthy.

"This happened overnight," Ki said, stepping forward. "Coinciding with Marcus's rally at the Innovation Center."

"Correlation isn't causation," Councilor Nakai countered. "Maybe people in the eastern quarter are just... disconnecting naturally. It's a new development. People haven't formed deep roots yet."

"Bonds don't sever cleanly," Ki said, frustration creeping into her voice. "When connections fade naturally, they thin gradually. These were cut. Deliberately."

"By Marcus Salter," Councilor Nakai said flatly. "A respected member of this community. A former Agricultural Council member. A man whose grandfather was a knowledge keeper."

"A man who went to the Corrupted Border ten years ago and came back changed," Aya said.

That got reactions. Several council members shifted uncomfortably. The Corrupted Border—the old industrial site at Kituhwa's edge where toxic waste had been dumped before the nation was established—was not a comfortable topic.

"We purified that land," Councilor Nakai said.

"We tried," Aya corrected. "But some corruption goes too deep. And Marcus went there alone, without protection, without ceremony. Whatever was in that soil got into him."

"Do you hear yourself?" Councilor Nakai stood, his voice rising. "You're accusing a prominent citizen of being possessed by evil spirits. This is exactly the kind of thinking that makes young people want to leave Kituhwa. Superstition masquerading as tradition."

"It's not superstition when I can feel it!" Ki's voice cracked. "I touch people in the eastern quarter and there's nothing there. No connection to their families. No bonds to their community. They look at each other like strangers who happen to share space. That's not natural disconnection. That's supernatural severance."

"Or," Councilor Nakai said icily, "it's what happens when people finally choose freedom over obligation."

The chamber went silent.

Elder Takoda raised a weathered hand. "Explain yourself, Nakai."

Councilor Nakai took a breath, seeming to gather himself. "I apologize for my tone. But we need to be honest about what's happening here. The Ascension Initiative isn't fringe. It's not a few malcontents. It's hundreds of our citizens—maybe thousands—asking legitimate questions about how we organize our society."

He gestured to the holographic display. "Maybe people in the eastern quarter aren't severed. Maybe they're finally free. Free to choose their connections instead of having them mandated by ceremony."

"You sound like him," Zola said, speaking for the first time. Her voice was low, dangerous. "You sound exactly like Marcus."

Councilor Nakai flinched. "I sound like someone who has a daughter. My daughter. Who wants to study biotechnology at a university outside Kituhwa. A university that does cutting-edge research we can't do here because our traditional agricultural practices limit experimentation."

He turned to face the other council members. "I told her she should stay. Keep the traditions. Honor the old ways. And you know what she said? She said, 'Dad, I love our culture. But I also love science. Why do I have to choose?'"

The question hung in the air.

"She doesn't," Ki said quietly. "Tradition and innovation aren't enemies."

"Aren't they?" Councilor Nakai pulled up his own data—statistics on youth retention, economic growth limitations, restrictions on external partnerships. "We have rules about how much we can trade with outside territories. Limits on technology adoption. Requirements for ceremonial participation. All in the name of tradition."

"All to maintain the sacred balance," Aya countered. "The balance that makes Kituhwa work."

"Does it?" Councilor Nakai's voice gentled. "Aya, I respect you. I respect your family's legacy. But maybe—just maybe—Kituhwa works because we have good agricultural practices, sustainable technology, and strong community bonds. Maybe the ceremonies are... adjacent to our success, not the cause of it."

Before Aya could respond, the chamber doors opened.

Marcus Salter walked in.

He hadn't been invited. Council sessions were open to the public, but there was an understanding about interrupting proceedings. Marcus ignored it, striding to the speaker's circle with the confidence of someone who knew exactly how this would play.

"Forgive the intrusion," Marcus said, his voice carrying that same persuasive warmth from his rally. "I heard my name being discussed and thought I should address the concerns directly."

Elder Takoda studied him with eyes that had seen seven decades of Kituhwa's history. "Marcus Salter. You were once a promising student of the traditional ways."

"I was," Marcus agreed. "And I'm grateful for that education. It gave me the foundation to understand why we need to evolve beyond it."

Zola moved, subtle but clear—positioning herself between Marcus and the elders. Her protective instinct screaming that this man was a threat.

Marcus noticed, smiled at her. "Hello, Zola. Still playing bodyguard?"

"Still protecting what matters," Zola said flatly.

Ki stepped forward, her gift active. She looked at Marcus and saw... nothing. Where there should have been bonds—connections to family, friends, community, even enemies—there was just void. Darkness. Absence.

"What happened to you?" Ki whispered.

Marcus's smile didn't waver. "I woke up."

He turned to address the full council. "Honored elders, fellow council members, I want to be direct. Yes, I'm encouraging people to question whether they truly need ceremonial bonds. Yes, I'm asking them to consider whether connection should be chosen rather than imposed. And yes, some people are finding that when they examine their relationships honestly, they're not as deep as ceremony made them seem."

"You're cutting their bonds," Aya said. "Supernaturally. We have evidence."

"You have interpretations," Marcus corrected gently. "You see people disconnecting and assume it's dark magic. I see people disconnecting and recognize it's choice."

He pulled up his own holographic display—survey data, testimonials, statistics on reported happiness in the eastern quarter. "People report feeling lighter. Less burdened. More free to pursue their own interests without guilt about ceremonial obligations."

"They report feeling alone," Ki countered, pulling up different data—her own records of bond degradation, psychological assessments showing increased isolation.

"Alone and lonely aren't the same thing," Marcus said. "Solitude can be freedom."

"Or it can be death," Aya said. "The Three Sisters don't grow alone. Nothing grows alone."

Marcus's expression hardened, just slightly. "The Three Sisters. Always the Three Sisters. Tell me, Aya—who decided corn, beans, and squash should define how an entire nation organizes itself?"

"Generations of ancestors," Aya said. "Centuries of wisdom. Practical experience showing that cooperation beats isolation."

"In agriculture," Marcus said. "The Three Sisters are good farming practice. But we've turned a planting technique into a political philosophy. We've made a metaphor into a mandate."

He addressed the council again. "I'm not anti-tradition. I'm anti-coercion. If people want to participate in ceremonies, beautiful. If they want to maintain strong community bonds, excellent. But it should be choice, not requirement."

"And what you're doing," Ki said, her voice tight, "what you're planning with this Unbinding ceremony—that's not giving people choice. That's taking away their ability to feel connection at all."

Something flickered in Marcus's eyes. For just a moment, Ki thought she saw fear there. Genuine fear. Like he was trapped in his own body, screaming.

Then it passed, and Marcus was smiling again.

"The Unbinding will let people choose their bonds consciously," he said. "Instead of having them imposed by ceremony, they'll forge them deliberately. Stronger connections, freely made."

"That's not how it works," Ki said desperately. "Bonds aren't contracts you negotiate. They're living things that grow naturally when people share experiences and care for each other."

"Sounds like a beautiful theory," Marcus said. "Let's test it."

He turned to leave, then paused at the chamber door.

"Tomorrow night," he said. "The Unbinding ceremony. At the Festival grounds. Everyone is invited. Come see what freedom looks like."

He walked out, and the chamber erupted in argument.

Half the council wanted Marcus arrested immediately. Half wanted to attend his ceremony out of curiosity or support. All of them were divided in ways that would have been unthinkable a month ago.

Elder Takoda raised his hand for silence. It took several minutes to achieve.

"Three Sisters," he said finally. "What do you recommend?"

Aya, Ki, and Zola looked at each other. They'd worked together for three years as guardians and protectors. They'd stopped external threats, mediated community disputes, and maintained the sacred balance. But they'd never faced something like this.

"We stop him," Aya said.

"How?" Councilor Nakai demanded. "He hasn't broken any laws. He's expressing political opinions and organizing a ceremony. That's protected activity."

"He's using supernatural powers to harm people," Zola said.

"Prove it," Councilor Nakai shot back. "Prove it in a way that doesn't rely on your interpretation of spiritual experiences."

Silence.

They couldn't. Not in a way that would satisfy someone who'd already decided to doubt.

"We need time," Ki said. "Time to investigate properly. Time to gather evidence. Time to understand exactly what Marcus is doing and how to counter it."

"You have until tomorrow night," Elder Takoda said. "After that, the Unbinding happens, and the people will choose. Either they choose connection, or they choose what Marcus offers."

"And if they choose wrong?" Aya asked.

The old man's expression was grave. "Then Kituhwa will learn a very old lesson about what happens when the Three Sisters stop growing together."

The council session ended in discord. As people filed out, the Three Sisters stood together in the speaker's circle, feeling the weight of an impossible task.

"We need to split up," Aya said. "Cover more ground. I'll investigate the Corrupted Border, find out exactly what happened to Marcus. Ki, you work on strengthening the bonds he's attacking. Zola, you track his movements, keep him from causing more damage before tomorrow."

Ki hesitated. "Split up? Isn't that exactly what we tell everyone not to do? The Three Sisters work together."

"We don't have time to do this together," Aya said. "We have less than twenty-four hours."

Zola cracked her knuckles. "Then let's move."

But as they separated—Aya heading east, Ki heading west, Zola heading north—none of them felt the confidence they'd projected.

For the first time since becoming the Three Sisters, they were working apart.

And somewhere in Kituhwa, Marcus Salter smiled, feeling their bonds weaken as distance grew between them.

*Exactly as planned.*

## CHAPTER THREE: IMANI'S WARNING

Ki's youth center occupied a converted warehouse in Kituhwa's arts district, a neighborhood where traditional weavers worked next to digital artists, where ceremonial dancers practiced in studios above graphic design firms. The building's exterior was covered in murals depicting creation stories, historical moments, and contemporary social commentary all flowing together in riotous color.

Inside, it was organized chaos.

Teenagers clustered around tables working on art projects, homework, and what looked like a complicated board game involving tribal trade routes. Younger kids played in a supervised corner, their laughter bouncing off the high ceilings. A few adults sat in conversation circles, processing emotions or planning community events. Music played from somewhere—contemporary R&B with traditional flute samples woven through it.

This was Ki's domain. The place where she built bonds one conversation at a time.

Usually, it felt alive with connection. Green threads visible to her gift, linking every person in the space through friendship, mentorship, shared creativity, and genuine care.

Tonight, the threads were fraying.

Ki stood in the center of the main room, her gift fully active, watching in horror as bonds she'd spent years building simply... came apart. Two teenage girls who'd been best friends for five years suddenly couldn't remember why they liked each other. A boy who'd been making steady progress on healing from family trauma looked around like he'd never seen the place before. Three adults in a conversation circle stopped mid-sentence, confused about why they were even there.

"No, no, no," Ki whispered, rushing from person to person, trying to restore what was breaking.

She touched the two girls, pushing green energy through them, reminding them of shared memories and inside jokes. The bonds reformed, tentative but real.

Then they broke again.

Cut clean through by something she couldn't see but could definitely feel. Like invisible scissors snipping away at her work.

Marcus. Even from a distance, his power was reaching into her center, her sanctuary, cutting away at everything she'd built.

"Ki?" One of her volunteers, a young man named Jesse, approached with concern. "What's happening? Everyone's acting weird."

Ki looked at Jesse and saw his bonds degrading too. The connection between him and his younger brother, who was in the kids' corner. The friendship he had with another volunteer. Even the casual bonds with the teens he mentored—all of them thinning like spider silk burning away.

"Jesse, I need you to take everyone home," Ki said, trying to keep the panic out of her voice. "Right now. Call their families. Get them out of here."

"But—"

"Please."

Something in her tone cut through his confusion. Jesse nodded and started coordinating, pulling out his phone to contact parents and guardians.

Ki watched people leave, watched them walk out into a night where they'd be even more vulnerable to whatever Marcus was doing. But keeping them here, in a place he was specifically targeting, felt worse.

The center emptied quickly, leaving Ki alone in the too-large space.

She sat down hard on one of the couches, her head in her hands. Her gift showed her the full scope of the damage: bonds breaking all across Kituhwa. Not just here, not just in the eastern quarter, but spreading like infection. Every person who'd attended Marcus's rally, every person who'd listened to his ideas, every person who'd even considered that maybe tradition was too demanding—they were all vulnerable.

And Marcus's power slipped into that vulnerability like poison into a wound.

"You're the Bean Sister."

Ki's head snapped up.

A young woman stood in the doorway—one Ki hadn't heard enter. She was nineteen or twenty, with long locs decorated with shells and beads, wearing a paint-stained vintage band shirt and ripped jeans. She held a worn canvas bag over one shoulder and had the intense look of someone who'd spent too many nights awake, chasing a truth nobody wanted to hear.

"I'm Kiona," Ki said carefully. "The center's closed for tonight."

"I'm Imani Ashanti," the young woman said, stepping inside. "And I need to talk to all three of you. Where are Aya and Zola?"

"They're investigating—" Ki stopped. "How do you know our names?"

"My father knew you." Imani's voice caught slightly. "Before he died."

She moved into the room, and Ki's gift automatically reached out, trying to read her bonds. What she saw made her breath catch.

Imani's connections were strange. Tangled. She had the normal bonds you'd expect—family, friends, community—but layered over them was a fine, gray dust, like the ash Marcus carried. But on Imani, the dust wasn't cutting the bonds. It was *coating* them. Protecting them? Or infecting them?

"My father was Dr. Aris Ashanti," Imani said, watching Ki's face. "He was the lead researcher on the Corrupted Border reclamation project ten years ago. The one who went in with Marcus Salter."

Ki went still. The official story was that Marcus went in alone. That he was the hero who risked everything.

"I see you know the lie," Imani said, her voice bitter. "Marcus didn't go alone. He went with a team of six. My father was one of them. Marcus is the only one who came back."

"What happened to them?" Ki asked, though she was afraid of the answer.

"They didn't die," Imani said. "That would have been kinder. Marcus... he found something in the deep soil. An old hunger. He didn't fight it. He made a deal with it. And he paid for that power with the souls of his team."

Imani reached into her bag and pulled out a jagged piece of black obsidian. It hummed with a low, nauseating vibration.

"This is all that's left of my father," she said. "His bond-stone. It should be glowing with his memory. Instead..." She held it out. It was cold and dead, absorbing the light around it.

"Marcus isn't trying to free people," Imani said, her eyes burning. "He's harvesting them. The Unbinding isn't about breaking bonds—it's about severing the connection to the earth so he can feed that hunger without resistance. Every bond he cuts is a meal for the thing living inside him."

Ki felt the bile rise in her throat. "He's going to do it to the whole city tomorrow."

"Unless you stop him," Imani said. "But you can't do it apart. He separated you on purpose. He knows Aya is strong enough to ground him, he knows Zola is strong enough to shield against him, and he knows you..."

Imani stepped closer. "He fears you the most, Ki."

"Me?" Ki shook her head. "I'm just the connector. I don't have Aya's power or Zola's strength."

"You have the network," Imani said intense. "You're the only one who can weave the bonds back together faster than he can cut them. That's why he attacked your center first. To make you doubt. To make you run."

Ki looked around the empty warehouse. She had run. She'd sent everyone away.

"You need to find your sisters," Imani said. "And you need to take this." She pressed the black stone into Ki's hand.

"What is it?"

"Evidence," Imani said. "And a warning. If he touches you, if he tries to cut you... let him touch this instead."

Ki closed her fingers around the cold stone. It felt heavy, burdened with grief, but also solid. Real.

"Thank you," Ki said.

"Don't thank me yet," Imani shouldered her bag. "Just save the city. I have to go—if he senses I'm here, he'll come for me."

She slipped out as quickly and quietly as she'd arrived.

Ki stood alone in the silence for exactly three seconds. Then she grabbed her comms unit.

"Aya, Zola," she broadcasted on their secure frequency. "Do not engage alone. Repeat, do not engage. It's a trap. I have proof."

Static.

"Aya? Zola?"

Nothing but white noise.

Marcus was already jamming them.

Ki ran for the door. She knew where Aya was going—the Corrupted Border. If Marcus had set a trap, that's where the teeth would be.

## CHAPTER FOUR: THE HUNGER IN THE SOIL

The Corrupted Border didn't look like a place where monsters lived. It looked like a parking lot.

Specifically, it was the cracked, overgrown remains of the old industrial shipping depot that had operated here before the Great Turning. Rusted shipping containers were stacked like rotting teeth against the twilight sky. Weeds forced their way through the concrete, but they weren't the lush, vibrant green of Kituhwa. They were pale, sickly yellow, growing in twisted spirals that hurt the eyes.

Aya walked through the rusting maze, her bare feet registering the shift in the earth. The hum of the Root Network, usually so constant and comforting, died here. It didn't fade; it was strangled.

Every step she took felt like walking into a vacuum. The soil beneath the concrete wasn't just dead; it was *hungry*.

"I know you're here," Aya said aloud. Her voice sounded flat, stripped of its resonance by the dampening field of the place.

She wasn't speaking to Marcus. She was speaking to the land itself.

Or rather, the thing that occupied it.

She knelt near a patch of exposed earth where the concrete had shattered. This was Ground Zero. The place where the records said the old chemical leaks had been worst. The place where Marcus and his team had supposedly performed their cleansing ritual ten years ago.

Aya placed her palms on the gray dirt.

Usually, when she did this, she felt life. Roots, bugs, water, memory.

This time, she felt a mouth.

*FEED.*

The psychic scream was so loud it knocked her backward. Aya gasped, scrambling away as the dirt itself seemed to ripple.

"You're not a spirit," she whispered, realizing the truth. "You're a wound."

The ground wasn't possessed. It was traumatized. Decades of poisoning, of extraction, of abuse had left a void that wanted to be filled. And Marcus... Marcus hadn't healed it. He had offered it a different food source.

Human connection.

"Smart," a voice drifted from the shadows of a container stack. "Most people think it's a demon. But you always did understand the earth, Aya."

Aya scrambled to her feet, shifting into a defensive stance. "Where are you, Marcus?"

"I'm not here," the voice said, echoing from everywhere at once. "I'm everywhere. I'm in the bonds I cut. I'm in the fear I sow. I'm in the freedom I offer."

A holographic projection flickered to life in front of her—a pre-recorded message, triggered by her presence. Marcus, looking younger, tired, standing in this exact spot.

"If you're seeing this," the recording said, "then you figured out where I went. You're probably disgusted. You're probably thinking about the Three Sisters and the Sacred Balance."

Recorded-Marcus laughed, a dry, humorless sound.

"But you don't know what it's like, Aya. To feel the pain of the land so acutely that it drives you mad. I didn't come here to gain power. I came here to stop the screaming. The earth here... it was in agony. It needed something to soothe it."

"So you fed it your friends," Aya said to the ghost.

"I gave it what it needed," the recording continued. "Connection. The raw energy of human bonds. It's the only thing that fills the void. And in return... it gave me the power to free everyone else."

The recording flickered and died.

Aya looked around. The shadows were lengthening, stretching toward her like fingers.

"He's not just feeding the land," she realized. "He's building a battery."

The severed bonds from the eastern quarter weren't just gone. The energy was being funneled here. Stored. Ready to be unleashed.

But why?

A tremor shook the ground. Not an earthquake. A heartbeat.

*THUMP-THUMP.*

It was coming from beneath the central stack of containers.

Aya moved toward it, her fear replaced by cold determination. She had to sever the link between Marcus and this place. She had to perform a grounding ritual so powerful it would force the hunger back into sleep.

She reached the center. There, in a cleared circle of dead earth, stood a totem. It wasn't traditional. It was a grotesque mockery—a structure made of bone and wire and black obsidian, wrapped in copper coils.

It was humming.

Aya reached out to topple it.

"I wouldn't do that," Zola's voice crackled over her comms, suddenly cutting through the static. "It's rigged."

Aya froze. "Zola? I thought comms were down."

"I'm barely getting through," Zola grunted. "Listen to me. I'm at the Innovation Center. I found his schematics. The totem at the Border isn't the source. It's the trigger."

"Trigger for what?"

"If you disturb it, it sends a feedback loop through the entire Root Network," Zola said. "It'll kill every plant in Kituhwa instantly. The crops. The gardens. The Sacred Fields. Everything dies."

Aya snatched her hand back as if the totem were red hot. "He's holding the land hostage."

"He wants you to try to fix it," Zola said. "He wants you to pour your energy into it. He'll drain you dry, Aya. Get out of there."

"I can't just leave it!"

"You have to," Zola insisted. "We have to regroup. Ki found something. We have a way to fight him, but we need all three of us."

Aya looked at the hungry earth, at the vibrating totem that threatened everything she loved. It went against every instinct she had to walk away from a wound in the world.

*FEED,* the ground whispered.

"Not today," Aya spat.

She turned and ran, her bare feet flying over the broken concrete, racing the setting sun back to her sisters.

Behind her, the shadows watched her go, and the hunger waited for tomorrow.
